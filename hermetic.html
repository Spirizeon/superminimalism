Why hermetic matters

The word hermetic didn't start in a build system. It started with Hermes Trismegistus, the mythical figure alchemists credited with the line "as above, so below". Alchemists would seal their vessels airtight so nothing could enter and nothing could escape – a closed world where the transformation inside depended only on what you put in. They called these seals hermetic seals.

Three thousand years later, engineers stole the word and used it for something surprisingly similar. A hermetic build is a build that sees exactly what you declared and nothing else – no network, no stray system packages, no half-configured `$HOME` leaking in. Same inputs, same outputs, forever. And somewhere between the alchemist's flask and the CI pipeline, I realised these patterns map uncomfortably well onto how to live a life.

~ the beauty of local-only restrictions

People hear "no network access" and think limitation. It's the opposite – it's honesty. A hermetic build stops lying to you. It can't quietly pull a dependency from the internet at 3 AM and pretend everything is fine. Every byte it consumes was written down somewhere, by someone, on purpose.

There's something deeply calming about a process that cannot surprise you. In security we call this reducing the attack surface; in life the Stoics called it the inner citadel – Marcus Aurelius sealing his judgments away from things he couldn't control. You don't control the network. You control your inputs. Guard them like a build tool does.

~ reproducibility, or killing "works on my machine"

Bazel is Google's answer to a question that haunted large engineering orgs: if two people run the same build on the same commit, why do they get different binaries? Bazel's answer is hermeticity at scale – pin every toolchain, hash every input, cache every artifact. Run it on your laptop or a datacenter in Singapore, you get the same bits back. Bit. For. Bit.

I've lost nights of my life to bugs that existed only on my machine. Flaky tests that pass in isolation and fail in CI. Every single one was an unsealed vessel – some hidden input nobody declared. Reproducibility isn't about speed. It's about being able to say: given this exact set of circumstances, this outcome was inevitable.

~ declarativeness and Starlark's discipline

Starlark is the language Bazel files are written in, and it's Python's most disciplined cousin. No file I/O while evaluating. No randomness. No threads. You literally cannot be clever with it – only clear. You declare what you want, never how to get it. The build graph becomes a fact, not a procedure.

This is where it stops being about code for me. The Bhagavad Gita calls it nishkama karma – action without attachment to the fruits of action. Do your duty, declare your intent, and release your grip on the outcome. That's not passivity. That's the most declarative sentence ever written: state the goal, surrender the execution.

~ nix: your whole OS as a pure function

If Bazel hermeticises a project, Nix hermeticises existence itself. Every package lives at a path hashed from its exact inputs in /nix/store. Upgrade breaks your setup? Roll back the entire machine like git checkout for reality. Your operating system stops being a pile of state accumulated over years and becomes a function you can evaluate again tomorrow and get today back.

I switched my dotfiles over and the first thing I felt wasn't productivity. It was peace. Nothing on my system is accidental anymore. Everything that exists, exists because a file said it should.

~ as above, so below: life as a hermetic build

Here's the inversion that keeps me up at night (yes, past 02:00 AM, with coffee). Japan has a phrase, ichi-go ichi-e – "one time, one meeting." Every tea ceremony, every conversation, happens exactly once and can never be reproduced. Life is the one build that is not hermetic. You cannot pin the inputs. You cannot re-run it. The environment drifts every second whether you declared it or not.

And that's precisely why the practices matter. The Stoics sealed their inputs – opinions, effort, judgment were theirs; reputation, outcomes, other people were not. Karma yoga declared the action and released the result. Memento mori is just the Romans reading the build logs: every input is finite, the final seal closes on all of us. Even wabi-sabi – the love of the cracked, unfinished thing – reads to me like making peace with a non-reproducible build instead of rage-quitting over it.

Different cultures, thousands of years apart, arrived at the same design review: control what enters the vessel. be deliberate about what you declare. accept what comes out.

~ the end?

Hermes had it right three thousand years before CI pipelines. As above, so below: the sealed flask and the sealed self obey the same rule.

pin your inputs. release your outcomes.
